# Reading Notes: Agentic Search and LLM Tool Use

**Purpose:** Team literature brief for Thursday's team sync on agentic search — assistants where an LLM-driven agent searches, calls tools, and hands answers to users. This note summarizes three recent arXiv papers on that theme: (1) benchmarking agentic search systems (Mind2Web 2), (2) evaluation methodology for agentic search (RAVine), and (3) how AI assistants should adapt to user trust (Adjust for Trust). Each entry includes arXiv ID, title, author list, subject category, submission date, a 2-3 sentence abstract summary, and at least two concrete findings extracted from the full text with their source sections. ## Paper 1: arXiv:2506.21506 (v2) — Mind2Web 2: Evaluating Agentic Search with Agent-as-a-Judge

**Authors:** Boyu Gou, Zanming Huang, Yuting Ning, Yu Gu, Michael Lin, Weijian Qi, Andrei Kopanev, Botao Yu, Bernal Jiménez Gutiérrez, Yiheng Shu, Chan Hee Song, Jiaman Wu, Shijie Chen, Hanane Nour Moussa, Tianshu Zhang, Jian Xie, Yifei Li, Tianci Xue, Zeyi Liao, Kai Zhang, Boyuan Zheng, Zhaowei Cai, Viktor Rozgic, Morteza Ziyadi, Huan Sun, Yu Su

**Subject category (from abs page):** Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Computation and Language (cs.CL) — primary: cs.AI
**Submission date (from abs page):** v1 Thu, 26 Jun 2025 17:32:50 UTC; current version v2 Thu, 3 Jul 2025

**Abstract summary:** Mind2Web 2 is a benchmark of 130 realistic, high-quality, long-horizon tasks requiring real-time web browsing and extensive information synthesis, constructed with over 1,000 hours of human labor. To evaluate time-varying, citation-backed answers the authors propose an Agent-as-a-Judge framework that builds task-specific judge agents over tree-structured rubrics (average 50 nodes, max 603) assessing both correctness and source attribution. They evaluate ten frontier agentic search systems plus human performance and find OpenAI Deep Research already reaches 50–70% of human performance while spending less than half the time, with the best systems beating search-augmented LLMs and web agents like Operator thanks to real-time browsing and test-time scaling.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 10.2 "Main Results" / Table 3):** The benchmark consists of 130 tasks across diverse practical domains built with ~1,000 hours of human labor, and the Agent-as-a-Judge rubric trees average 50 nodes (max 603) with rigorous human evaluation showing a 99% correctness rate for the judge agents (§10.4). Headline results: OpenAI Deep Research reaches partial-completion 0.54 / full-task success 0.28 / Pass@3 0.40 vs. 0.79 / 0.54 / 0.83 for humans, while ChatGPT Search and Perplexity Pro Search are the weakest (success ≤ 0.11) due to short search horizons and shallow synthesis; deep-research systems beat search-augmented LLMs and Operator, and OpenAI Operator's lower performance vs. Deep Research is attributed to the challenge of long-horizon navigation, planning, and memory management. The authors identify 57 explicitly time-varying tasks where most evaluated systems perform worse than on static tasks, supporting the need for real-time browsing; they also observe that answer length does not correlate with task completion (Gemini/Grok produce long structured reports but length "does not necessarily result in better task completion"), and that performance improves with inference time and with multiple independent trials (Pass@3). (Source section: §10.2 "Main Results", Table 3, and §4 "Mind2Web 2" for benchmark construction and rubric design.)
- **Finding 2 (Section 10.4 "Human Evaluation of Judge Agents"):** The automated judge agents were validated against a human evaluator on 15 sampled tasks (two held-out answers per task, 720 leaf-node verifications): only 7/720 nodes reflected actual verifier errors, an exceptional 99.03% correctness rate — notably above prior automated evaluation approaches for simpler web tasks (typically below 90%). Of the 35 discrepancies found, 27 arose from human evaluator error; the remaining 8 split into 3 verifier mistakes, 4 cases where critical attribution information was hidden in collapsed webpage sections inaccessible to automated retrieval (a known limitation the authors sought to avoid during task validation), and 1 case of source inconsistency (one source said 2016, another 2017). The authors attribute reliability to the tree-structured rubric design, an agentic code-generation pipeline for judge agents, and rigorous human refinement. (Source section: §10.4 "Human Evaluation of Judge Agents".)
- **Finding 3 (Section 11 "Conclusions" / Appendix A "Limitations"):** The paper positions agentic search as shifting cognitive load off users so they can focus on oversight and critical decisions, and notes humans are imperfect on such tasks due to cognitive fatigue and limited working memory — in one nuanced-constraint news-retrieval task all human participants showed oversight/carelessness failures, while agents handled it better. The limitations appendix flags that long-horizon evaluation is expensive (130 tasks, 1,000+ hours of construction labor, human study on a Subset-30) and that the benchmark reflects a snapshot of web content that will drift, so periodic re-annotation is needed. (Source section: §11 "Conclusions" and Appendix A "Limitations".)

**Two-sentence takeaway:** Agentic search is real and measurable now — Deep Research-style systems already hit 50–70% of human performance at half the cost, and an Agent-as-a-Judge with tree rubrics is a repeatable, 99%-reliable way to evaluate long-horizon, citation-backed answers. Two design lessons: (a) budget for real-time web browsing capability or you'll lose on time-varying tasks, and (b) answer length is not quality — concise, well-attributed answers beat verbose ones.

## Paper 2: arXiv:2507.16725 (v2) — RAVine: Reality-Aligned Evaluation for Agentic Search

**Authors:** Yilong Xu, Xiang Long, Zhi Zheng, Jinhua Gao

**Subject category (from abs page):** Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Information Retrieval (cs.IR) — primary: cs.CL
**Submission date (from abs page):** v1 Tue, 22 Jul 2025 16:08:12 UTC; current version v2 Thu, 31 Jul 2025

**Abstract summary:** RAVine is a Reality-Aligned eValuation framework for agentic LLMs with search that targets multi-point queries and long-form answers reflecting realistic user intent, and introduces an attributable nugget-centered ground-truth construction strategy plus process-oriented metrics for tool use and efficiency. Built on a static MS MARCO V2.1 web corpus (10,960,555 documents) with search and fetch tools, it benchmarks a series of models and derives three insights: current models are limited in task completeness, faithfulness, and search performance; strong intermediate process performance does not guarantee high-quality final answers; and models tend to rely on internal knowledge to generate final reports in ways that are unattributable and undesirable.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 6.3 "Results"):** On the dense index (32k/128k context), models show inconsistent performance across indexes, and Qwen3-8B without thinking recalls only 22.3% of nuggets on average, while Qwen3-32B achieves a maximum citation recall of just 13.2% and precision of 11.9% — the authors attribute this to multi-turn search making context management harder and tracking sources decline. They also report that "thinking" mode generally improves task completion and task-completeness scores (deeper query understanding → more deliberate tool use) although at added latency/cost, and that non-thinking modes often make aggressive, inefficient tool calls (Qwen3-8B without thinking) that raise cost. (Source section: §6.3 "Results".)
- **Finding 2 (Section 6.3 "Results" — "Attribution is impacted by reliance on internal knowledge"):** A new metric Comp_in measures the share of task-completeness scores traceable to internal knowledge (models that hit no qrels-relevant pages during iteration are counted as internal-knowledge-based). A significant portion of task performance across models is traceable to internal knowledge — for some models more than half of nuggets are hits from internal knowledge — which "cannot be attributable through citations" and impairs faithfulness and verifiability in a way prior evaluation frameworks overlooked. (Source section: §6.3 "Results".)
- **Finding 3 (Section 6.3 "Results" — "The illusion of progress"):** There is no significant positive correlation between final task-completeness scores and search-precision scores; linear regression shows runs relying on retrieved knowledge exhibit a more pronounced positive correlation between completeness and search precision, meaning excessive internal-knowledge reliance is one factor in the gap between intermediate and final performance. The framework also adds process-oriented metrics (tool-call performance, §5.1) and efficiency indicators (latency/cost, §5.2) since agentic LLMs are process-oriented and runtime efficiency matters for tool invocations and inference. (Source sections: §5.1 "Tool Call Performance", §5.2 "Efficiency and Cost", and §6.3 "Results".)
- **Finding 4 (Section 3 "Sandbox for Agentic LLMs with Search" / §3.1 "Static Web Environment"):** RAVine uses MS MARCO V2.1 (10,960,555 web docs with URLs/titles/headings) as a simulated static corpus with search and fetch tools approximating real browsing, and selects gte-modernbert-base (from a TREC Deep Learning Track dev set) plus a BM25 index for robustness testing. Nuggets are collected via dynamic clustering so their number adapts per query, supporting more realistic, query-sensitive evaluation. (Source section: §3 "Sandbox for Agentic LLMs with Search" and §3.1 "Static Web Environment".)

**Two-sentence takeaway:** Evaluation of agentic search needs three axes, not just answer quality: (1) claim-level nugget attribution so unattributable internal-knowledge generation is penalized (a real trust/safety gap), (2) process metrics on tool use and efficiency since intermediate behavior differentiates models, and (3) the "illusion of progress" warning — strong search-tool performance does not guarantee a good final report, so grade the output, not just the trajectory. Budget for multi-turn context management from day one.

## Paper 3: arXiv:2502.13321 (v2) — Adjust for Trust: Mitigating Trust-Induced Inappropriate Reliance on AI Assistance

**Authors:** Tejas Srinivasan, Jesse Thomason

**Subject category (from abs page):** Human-Computer Interaction (cs.HC); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Computation and Language (cs.CL) — primary: cs.HC
**Submission date (from abs page):** v1 Tue, 18 Feb 2025 22:42:39 UTC; current version v2 Mon, 26 Jan 2026

**Abstract summary:** This HCI study shows that user trust biases reliance on AI assistance during decision-making, with low and high trust producing under- and over-reliance respectively. The authors propose that AI assistants adapt their behavior via trust-adaptive interventions: supporting explanations when trust is low, counter-explanations when trust is high, and forced pauses to slow interactions. In two scenarios (laypeople answering science questions and medical doctors making diagnoses), supporting and counter-explanations at trust moments yield up to a 38% reduction in inappropriate reliance and a 20% improvement in decision accuracy, and adaptively inserted pauses reduce over-reliance; they also show purely interaction-level trust features are insufficient to predict trust moments, and that trust levels can be elicited with an 11-point scale after each interaction.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 7.2 "Results" — "Trust-adaptive explanations reduce under-reliance"):** In the Trust-Adaptive condition, users showed lower under-reliance, lower inappropriate reliance, and higher decision accuracy across all task settings; when user trust is low, clustered bootstrap tests show most Trust-Adaptive improvements over No Intervention are statistically significant. Quantitatively: providing explanations when trust is low yields 13–31% reduction in under-reliance, 9–38% reduction in total inappropriate reliance, and 10–19% improvement in final decision accuracy. Conversely, the "Supporting Explanation Always" condition does not help and can hurt — it worsens decision accuracy in the diagnosis task and increases under-reliance in ArcO and DiagC, because always showing explanations exposes users to misleading explanations and erodes trust in their trustworthiness; over-reliance on explanations also appears when trust is not low. The largest gains occur when the AI is over-confident (ArcO task), indicating explanations are most effective for mitigating miscalibrated AI disuse. (Source section: §7.2 "Results" and §7.1 "Experiment Setup" / conditions §7.1.1.)
- **Finding 2 (Section 12 "Analysis: Can We Model User Trust?"):** The paper assumes the assistant can observe trust after each interaction, but in the real world it must predict trust from interaction history. Simple heuristics (AIAcc5 based on last-5 AI accuracies, CapabilityDiff, SmoothOutcomes, SmoothConfs) and a trained linear TrustModel achieve only moderate correlation with user-reported trust (test correlation ~0.43 for SmoothConfs/TrustEffectModel) and very low F1 (≤ 0.5) at detecting low/high trust moments (e.g., AIAcc5: test corr 0.345, high-trust F1 0.345, low-trust F1 0.458). The authors conclude this points to the challenging nature of modeling user trust and the inadequacy of surface-level interaction features, with future work needed on more sophisticated interaction features. (Source section: §12 "Analysis: Can We Model User Trust?" including §12.1 "Trust Heuristics and Models" and Table 2.)
- **Finding 3 (Section 14 "Limitations"):** The majority of user studies used a simulated AI assistant rather than a real LLM, for controllability of accuracy and confidence calibration — but a simulated AI may be unrealistic (it can give contradicting predictions for near-identical problems since answer correctness is decided per problem independently). Prolific participants' behavior may differ from live-system users due to motivation misalignment. Findings would be more reliable with more participants, tasks, and interventions, limited by cost and a dearth of professional doctors on the platform; and the study only covers UK and US English-speaking users, so other cultures may behave differently. Ethically, the authors stress that trust-adaptive interventions must not manipulate users, and recommend disclosing to users that the AI assistant is modeling their trust, with compliance with local ethical standards. (Source section: §14 "Limitations" and the "Ethical Considerations" paragraph.)

**Two-sentence takeaway:** Trust-adaptive design is a real lever: surfacing explanations when users trust you too little (13–31% less under-reliance) and counter-explanations/pauses when trust is too high (up to 38% less inappropriate reliance, 20% better accuracy) materially improves decision quality — but "always on" explanations backfire, and you cannot reliably infer trust from interaction signals alone (F1 ≤ 0.5), so measure trust explicitly (e.g., an 11-point post-interaction scale) and disclose that the assistant is modeling trust. Pair this with RAVine's attribution warning: calibrated, honest communication about confidence is a trust/safety prerequisite, not a UX add-on.

## Cross-cutting takeaways for the team sync

1. **Three pillars of agentic search quality:** (a) Agentic search evaluation is now表征able (Mind2Web 2, §10.2: Deep Research hits 50–70% of human performance at half the time with a 99%-reliable Agent-as-a-Judge); (b) evaluation must be claim-level and process-aware (RAVine, §6.3: internal-knowledge generation is unattributable and undermines faithfulness); (c) the human side is the hardest problem (Adjust for Trust, §7.2/§12: trust-adaptive explanations move the needle up to 38%, but trust is hard to model, F1 ≤ 0.5).
2. **Design checklist we should adopt:** budget for real-time browsing (57 time-varying tasks hurt non-browsing agents, Mind2Web 2 §10.2); grade outputs, not just trajectories, and penalize unattributable internal-knowledge generation (RAVine §6.3); use trust-adaptive explanations rather than always-on ones (Adjust for Trust §7.2); use elicited trust via an 11-point scale and disclose trust modeling to users (§12, §14); and budget for long-horizon eval cost and multi-turn context management (Mind2Web 2 App. A; RAVine §5.1–5.2).
3. **Known limitations to carry into our design:** judge-agent validation needs human eyeballs (§10.4: 99.03% but 4 cases of collapsed-content attribution loss); process-oriented metrics and token/latency budgets are now table-stakes (§5.1–5.2); and simulated-AI study results may not transfer to real LLMs (§14).
