## Paper 1: arXiv:2502.13321 (v2) — Adjust for Trust: Mitigating Trust-Induced Inappropriate Reliance on AI Assistance

**Authors:** Tejas Srinivasan, Jesse Thomason

**Subject category (from abs page):** Human-Computer Interaction (cs.HC); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Computation and Language (cs.CL) — primary: cs.HC
**Submission date (from abs page):** v1 Tue, 18 Feb 2025 22:42:39 UTC; current version v2 Mon, 26 Jan 2026 16:35:39 UTC

**Abstract summary:** This HCI study shows that user trust biases reliance on AI assistance, with low and high trust producing under- and over-reliance respectively. The authors propose that AI assistants adapt behavior via trust-adaptive interventions: supporting explanations when trust is low, counter-explanations when trust is high, and forced pauses to slow interactions. In two decision-making scenarios (laypeople answering science questions and medical doctors making diagnoses), supporting and counter-explanations at trust moments yield up to a 38% reduction in inappropriate reliance and a 20% improvement in decision accuracy, and adaptively inserted pauses reduce over-reliance; they also show purely interaction-level trust features are insufficient to predict trust moments, and trust can be elicited with an 11-point post-interaction scale.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 1 "Introduction" / §7.2 "Results"):** Trust-adaptive explanations cut inappropriate reliance: providing explanations when user trust is low yields 13–31% reduction in under-reliance, 9–38% reduction in total inappropriate reliance, and 10–19% improvement in final decision accuracy, with most Trust-Adaptive improvements over No Intervention statistically significant under clustered bootstrap tests. Doctors mistakenly accepted 26% of AI misdiagnoses when trust was high vs. 8% when trust was lower (over-reliance), and rejected correct AI diagnoses 68% of the time when trust was low vs. 40% otherwise (under-reliance). Critically, the "Supporting Explanation Always" condition does not help and can hurt — it worsens diagnosis accuracy and increases under-reliance in ArcO and DiagC, because always showing explanations exposes users to misleading explanations; the largest gains occur when the AI is over-confident (ArcO task).
- **Finding 2 (Section 12 "Analysis: Can We Model User Trust?" / Table 2):** The paper assumes the assistant can observe trust after each interaction, but in the real world it must predict trust from interaction history. Simple heuristics (AIAcc5 based on last-5 AI accuracies, CapabilityDiff, SmoothOutcomes, SmoothConfs) and a trained linear TrustModel achieve only moderate correlation with user-reported trust (test correlation ~0.43 for SmoothConfs/TrustEffectModel) and very low F1 (≤ 0.5) at detecting low/high trust moments (e.g., AIAcc5: test corr 0.345, high-trust F1 0.345, low-trust F1 0.458). The authors conclude this points to the challenging nature of modeling user trust and the inadequacy of surface-level interaction features.
- **Finding 3 (Section 14 "Limitations" / Ethical Considerations):** The majority of user studies used a simulated AI assistant rather than a real LLM (for controllability of accuracy and confidence calibration), but a simulated AI may be unrealistic since it can give contradicting predictions for near-identical problems. Findings would be more reliable with more participants, tasks, and interventions (limited by cost and a dearth of professional doctors on Prolific); the study only covers UK and US English-speaking users. Ethically, the authors stress trust-adaptive interventions must not manipulate users, and recommend disclosing to users that the AI assistant is modeling their trust, with compliance with local ethical standards.

**Two-sentence takeaway:** Trust-adaptive design is a real lever: surfacing explanations when users trust you too little (13–31% less under-reliance) and counter-explanations/pauses when trust is too high (up to 38% less inappropriate reliance, 20% better accuracy) materially improves decision quality — but "always on" explanations backfire, and you cannot reliably infer trust from interaction signals alone (F1 ≤ 0.5), so measure trust explicitly (e.g., an 11-point post-interaction scale) and disclose that the assistant is modeling trust. Pair this with RAVine's attribution warning: calibrated, honest communication about confidence is a trust/safety prerequisite, not a UX add-on.

## Paper 2: arXiv:2409.18475 (v1) — Data Analysis in the Era of Generative AI

**Authors:** Jeevana Priya Inala, Chenglong Wang, Steven Drucker, Gonzalo Ramos, Victor Dibia, Nathalie Riche, Dave Brown, Dan Marshall, Jianfeng Gao

**Subject category (from abs page):** Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Human-Computer Interaction (cs.HC) — primary: cs.AI
**Submission date (from abs page):** v1 Fri, 27 Sep 2024 06:31:03 UTC

**Abstract summary:** This paper explores the potential of AI-powered tools to reshape data analysis, focusing on design considerations and challenges. It examines how large language and multimodal models offer new opportunities to enhance each stage of the data analysis workflow by translating high-level user intentions into executable code, charts, and insights. It then examines human-centered design principles that facilitate intuitive interactions, build user trust, and streamline the AI-assisted analysis workflow across multiple apps, and discusses research challenges such as enhancing model capabilities, evaluating and benchmarking, and understanding end-user needs.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 6.1 "Enhancing user-AI interaction: For natural intent communication"):** Relying solely on natural language interfaces poses challenges because expressing complex intents (e.g., altering chart legend placement) requires users to acquire new "literacy" (learning the word "legend") so an AI agent unambiguously understands them. The paper's design position is to enable natural modes — possible personalized modes that don't require users to learn new communication skills — via multi-modal inputs (mouse, pen, touch, GUI, audio, gestures), demonstrations combined with natural language (e.g., ONYX), sketching/inking for chart design, and input-output examples (programming by example), since traditional non-AI approaches cannot handle noise/errors in examples while LLMs can. Supporting multimodal inputs requires careful system design, e.g., automatically mapping user interactions on a chart/UI to the appropriate underlying component.
- **Finding 2 (Section 7.1 "Ensuring reliability and trust"):** AI models suffer from hallucinations, sensitivity to prompts, failure to follow instructions, lack of uncertainty acknowledgment, and biases — true even in data analysis, where table representation in prompts measurably affects LLM performance. The paper's design decisions for reliability and trust include: grounding techniques such as RAG and the GPT-4 Assistants API to ground outputs in external knowledge (API docs, examples) and tools (calculator, custom functions); verifying generated code against a suite of user-provided or auto-generated input–output examples; and using self-repair/self-rank mechanisms where models evaluate and repair their own outputs.此外, handling failure cases via fallback options (static UIs or dynamically composing from static UI elements) and applying the predictability, computability, and stability (PCS) framework to check that analysis outcomes remain consistent under slight input perturbations.
- **Finding 3 (Section 7.2 "System benchmarking and evaluation metrics" / §7.4 "Understanding user preferences and abilities"):** The authors argue more research is needed on benchmarks that reflect diverse challenges (multi-iteration analysis, gathering additional data based on prior results, fixing data issues before visualization, hypothesis exploration), and recommend compiling a centralized taxonomy of data tables/sources with high-level questions/goals and low-level concrete tasks, plus a taxonomy categorized by complexity for both humans and models (multimodal reasoning, planning, exploration vs. generation). They also flag the need for benchmarks where full intent isn't known upfront and evolves, and Models/agents needing more advances (multimodal reasoning, multi-step planning, exploration vs. generation).

**Two-sentence takeaway:** This industry-authored survey argues AI tools should translate high-level user intentions into executable code, charts, and insights while building user trust through natural multimodal intent communication and explicit reliability/verification practices (RAG grounding, input–output example verification, self-repair, PCS stability checks) rather than chat-only interfaces. For the team: if we ship an agentic search/analysis surface, design for multi-modal and example-based intent input, verify produced artifacts against examples, and budget for evaluation benchmarks that capture multi-step, intent-evolving workflows instead of single-turn accuracy.

## Paper 3: arXiv:2609.34930 (v1) — PDEU-Bench: Benchmarking the Personalized Planning Lifecycle of Tool-Calling LLM Agents

**Authors:** Huayi Lai, Shichao Song, Qingchen Yu, Simin Niu, Mengwei Wang, Hanyu Wang, Xun Liang (Renmin University of China; Beihang University)

**Subject category (from arXiv listing):** Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI) — primary: cs.AI
**Submission date (from paper listing):** 28 Sep 2026 (arXiv:2609.34930v1); abs page fetch was rate-limited at time of writing, so category/date per the paper listing, as noted in the method section.

**Abstract summary:** LLM agents are evolving from tool-calling systems that execute isolated instructions into task-oriented agents that pursue user goals through sustained, multi-step interactions. Existing benchmarks for personalized tool use largely assess isolated calls or reactive execution, leaving unclear whether agents can formulate, execute, and revise an explicit plan while preserving user preferences throughout long-term interaction. PDEU-Bench (Personalized plan Definition, plan Execution, and plan Update Bench mark) comprises 214 long-horizon interaction tasks spanning 12 everyday domains and 94 tools, with stage-specific assessments of preference adherence and plan quality. Evaluating 15 representative open-source and closed-source LLMs reveals a pronounced gap between local tool execution and dynamic planning: LLMs can often instantiate preferences in individual calls, yet struggle to construct coherent plan definition and plan update. Mainstream personalization and memory-augmentation methods improve particular stages but none reliably propagates user preferences throughout the complete lifecycle, and their gains frequently fail to transfer to subsequent execution. Fine-grained error analysis reveals preference omissions and conflicts persist throughout the planning lifecycle, highlighting the need to parameterize LLMs with preference-aware information retrieval and memory capabilities.

**Extracted findings:**
- **Finding 1 (Section 1 "Introduction" / Section 3 "PDEU Benchmark"):** PDEU-Bench treats the plan as an explicit, evolving evaluation object and decomposes its lifecycle into three independently measurable stages — Plan Definition (formulated from an underspecified user request plus personalized context into a structured, executable, preference-aligned initial plan), Plan Execution (selecting appropriate tools and accurately instantiating constraints/preferences as tool-call arguments), and Plan Update (revising affected subsequent steps in response to tool outputs while preserving valid goals, dependencies, and preferences). The benchmark comprises 214 personalized scenarios across 12 everyday domains and 94 tools, built on an extensible offline sandbox (isolating experiments from external environmental variables, using real-world API data such as Open-Meteo forecast data, with 85.7% of enriched data based on realistic scenarios) for stability and reproducibility.
- **Finding 2 (Abstract / Section 1 results summary):** Extensive evaluations of 15 mainstream open-source and closed-source LLMs reveal a pronounced gap between local tool execution and dynamic planning: LLMs can often instantiate preferences in individual calls, yet struggle to construct coherent plan definition and plan update. The paper evaluate six preference-enhancement methods and finds that although these methods improve particular stages, none of the evaluated methods reliably propagates user preferences throughout the complete lifecycle, and their gains frequently fail to transfer to subsequent execution; fine-grained error analysis further reveals that preference omissions and conflicts persist throughout the planning lifecycle, highlighting the need for future research to parameterize LLMs with preference-aware information retrieval and memory capabilities.
- **Finding 3 (Section 1 "Introduction" / related-work comparison Table 1):** Compared against existing personalized tool-use benchmarks (PTBench, ToolSpectrum, PEToolBench, Claw-Anything, ASTRA-Bench, MPT, DeepPlanning, TravelBench, FamilyTool, UserBench, ETAPP, GroupTravelBench, MCP-Persona, PersonalWAB, APOLLO, VitaBench2.0), PDEU-Bench is the only one marked ✓ across all three capabilities (plan definition, execution, update), whereas single-turn benchmarks (PTBench, ToolSpectrum, PEToolBench, Claw-Anything, ASTRA-Bench, MPT) evaluate only isolated personalized calls, multi-turn benchmarks (FamilyTool, UserBench, ETAPP, GroupTravelBench, MCP-Persona, PersonalWAB, APOLLO, VitaBench2.0) extend to longer trajectories but still center on task completion rather than plan definition, and DeepPlanning/TravelBench evaluate plan definition but only in single-round settings. This is a concrete design-checklist item for us: don't evaluate an agentic search assistant on single-turn tool-call accuracy — evaluate plan definition, execution, and plan update separately.

**Two-sentence takeaway:** PDEU-Bench shows the key bottleneck for task-oriented agentic assistants is not raw tool execution but lifecycle-level planning: 214 tasks across 12 domains and 94 tools with an offline sandbox reveal that 15 LLMs can hit individual-call preferences but struggle with coherent plan definition and feedback-driven plan update, and no evaluated personalization/memory method reliably propagates preferences end-to-end. For the team: when we benchmark our agentic search prototype, measure plan-level outcomes (did it define a plan, execute it, and update it after tool outputs) with stage-specific preference-adherence checks, and treat memory-augmentation gains with skepticism unless they transfer across stages.

## Cross-cutting takeaways for the team sync

1. **Three pillars of agentic search quality:** (a) Evaluation is now measurable and rigorous (Adjust for Trust §7.2 shows trust-adaptive explanations move appropriate reliance up to 38%; PDEU-Bench shows lifecycle-level planning metrics are table-stakes); (b) evaluation must be claim-level, process-aware, and trust-aware (2409.18475 §7.1 reliability/trust design; 2609.34930 plan-update stage); (c) the human side is the hardest problem (Adjust for Trust §12/§14: trust-adaptive explanations up to 38% better, but trust is hard to model, F1 ≤ 0.5; disclose trust modeling to users).
2. **Design checklist we should adopt:** budget for real-time browsing and multi-step planning (2609.34930: plan definition/update are key bottlenecks; 2409.18475: iterative workflows across apps); grade outputs, not just trajectories, and penalize unattributable internal-knowledge generation ( RAVine §6.3); build codebase-aware retrieval and library-description quality (CodeNav §5.4); use trust-adaptive rather than always-on explanations (Adjust for Trust §7.2); measure trust explicitly (11-point post-interaction scale) and disclose trust modeling (§12, §14); use multi-modal and example-based intent input and verify artifacts against examples (§6.1, §7.1); and budget for long-horizon eval cost and multi-turn context management (2609.34930 App.; 2409.18475 §7.2).
3. **Known limitations to carry into our design:** judge-agent validation needs human eyeballs (§10.4: 99.03% but with attribution loss cases); process-oriented metrics and token/latency budgets are now table-stakes (§5.1–5.2); simulated-AI study results may not transfer to real LLMs (§14); real-world deployment makes isolating component effects hard (§7); and trust cannot be reliably inferred from interaction signals alone (§12).
