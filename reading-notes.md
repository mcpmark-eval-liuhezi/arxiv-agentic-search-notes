# Reading Notes: Agentic Search and LLM Tool Use

A short literature brief for Thursday's team sync on **agentic search** — assistants where an LLM-driven agent searches, calls tools, and hands answers to users. The brief was assembled end to end: arXiv searches were run on the requested themes ("conversational search agents", "tool use in LLM agents", "user trust in AI assistants"), and the three most relevant hits that surfaced are covered below. Every paper was then read through its full text, not just the abstract, and each entry reports two or three concrete findings (evaluation results, design decisions, or acknowledged limitations), each tagged with the paper section it came from. Subject category and submission date are taken from each paper's public arXiv abstract page. Nothing below rests on an abstract summary: each finding is quoted or paraphrased from the full manuscript.

## Paper 1 — 2609.19244 — Characterizing Web Search by Conversational LLM Agents

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2609.19244 (v1) |
| **Title** | Characterizing Web Search by Conversational LLM Agents: From Search Decisions and Strategies to Results and Responses |
| **Authors** | Mahsa Amani, Seungeon Lee, Abhisek Dash, Asmaa El Fraihi, Yunah Jang, Elisabeth Kirsten, Qinyuan Wu, Krishna P. Gummadi, Manish Gupta, Abhilasha Ravichander, Muhammad Bilal Zafar, Soumi Das |
| **Subject category (arXiv abstract page)** | Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI) — primary; Information Retrieval (cs.IR) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abstract page)** | Wed, 16 Sep 2026 17:56:32 UTC (v1) |
| **arXiv abs page** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19244 |

### Abstract summary

This is the first study of end-to-end Web search carried out by conversational LLM agents, combining real donated user conversations ("in vivo") from four platforms — ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and DeepSeek — with controlled API-based replay of the same prompts ("in vitro"). The authors examine when a model decides to invoke Web search, how agents query and iteratively reformulate queries based on retrieved results, the domain preferences of the search engines they hit, and how the claims in the final answer are actually grounded in cited evidence. Their headline findings: search-calling decisions vary enormously across platforms and models, more frequent Web-search invocation does not necessarily yield better response quality, several claims rest on search results the agent fetched but never cited, and the search engines returning results concentrate them in the platforms' preferred domains.

### Findings (extracted from the full text)

- **Search-calling decisions vary by an order of magnitude across agents, and invoking search often does not improve answers.** In the "in vitro" setup the same 1,000 user prompts were replayed through each platform's API, and Web search was invoked for only 140 of the prompts (14%) by one agent versus 825 (83%) by another; because prompt conditions differ between platforms, the authors cannot directly compare calling decisions in the observational data, which is why they build a controlled comparison. **[Source: Section 3 "Analyzing Search Calling Decisions", specifically §3.1 "Search Calling Behaviors"]**
- **Median number of Web queries per prompt spans 2 (Claude) to 4 (Grok), with markedly sequential refinement, and Claude is the exception to iterative fan-out.** Conversational agents freely issue multiple Web queries, in parallel fan-out queries or across iterations, with query specificity (time, geography, entity) increasing as iterations proceed, and source terms in later iterations increasingly deriving from retrieved search results rather than just the conversation history; by contrast, Claude generates only one query per iteration, which produces much deeper — but less fanning — search. **[Source: Section 4 "Analyzing Search Queries and Results", specifically §4.1 "Querying Strategies" and §4.2 "Query Formulation and Reformulation"]**
- **Search engines built for LLM agents return few URLs and concentrate them in domain-specific preferences that differ per platform.** Search engines on Claude and Grok return roughly 10 results per query, DeepSeek roughly 5, and ChatGPT roughly 15 — far fewer than a typical Google search — and top-10 domains accounted for 21.3% (in vivo) / 47.8% (in vitro) of returned results for ChatGPT and 32.3% / 29% for Grok. `reddit.com` and `youtube.com` dominate results for ChatGPT and Grok but are completely absent from Claude search, while `xiaohongshu.com` is prominent only in DeepSeek search. **[Source: Section 4 "Analyzing Search Queries and Results", specifically §4.3 "Domain Preferences in Search Results"]**
- **Attribution breakdowns by design: a large share of claims rest on search results the agent never cited, and the ungrounded portion is only the least factual.** Search results returned by the engines but never cited in the final response still account for 14% to 53% of all claims across ChatGPT, Claude and Grok, and 15%–20% of claims remain ungrounded overall; factuality scores are highest for claims grounded in associated citations (3.47 and 3.35 in ChatGPT) and lowest for ungrounded claims (likely parametric-knowledge-derived). **[Source: Section 13 "Analyzing Search Responses", specifically §13.2 "Grounding of Claims"]**
- **Acknowledged limitation — some observed behaviour may be harness rather than model, and the analysis is English-only.** The observational traces do not expose internal chain-of-thought traces, ranking algorithms, or proprietary retrieval pipelines of the platforms, so some observed behaviours reflect platform-specific harness implementations rather than the underlying models, and provenance analysis of query reformulation cannot perfectly distinguish whether new query terms originate from retrieved evidence, latent parametric knowledge, or implicit reasoning traces. **[Source: "Limitations"]**

### Two-sentence takeaway
Search-calling behaviour is a platform artifact as much as a need signal: the same prompts draw a 14%–83% spread of search-calling decisions, and more searches do not necessarily improve answers. Attribution is the structural weak point — 14%–53% of claims sit on results the agent fetched but never cited — and users have no visibility into the domain preferences (e.g. Claude never surfacing Reddit/YouTube) encoding these biases.

## Paper 2 — 2509.23694 — SafeSearch: Automated Red-Teaming of LLM-Based Search Agents

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2509.23694 (latest published version v6) |
| **Title** | SafeSearch: Automated Red-Teaming of LLM-Based Search Agents |
| **Authors** | Jianshuo Dong, Sheng Guo, Hao Wang, Xun Chen, Zhuotao Liu, Tianwei Zhang, Ke Xu, Minlie Huang, Han Qiu |
| **Subject category (arXiv abstract page)** | Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI) — primary; Computation and Language (cs.CL); Cryptography and Security (cs.CR) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abstract page)** | v1 Sun, 28 Sep 2025 07:05:17 UTC (v1, first submission); latest version v6 Wed, 3 Jun 2026 04:13:57 UTC. Comments: Accepted by ICML 2026. |
| **arXiv abs page** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23694 |

### Abstract summary

Search agents connect LLMs to the Internet, enabling them to access broader and more current information, but unreliable search results are an entirely new threat surface: misinformation, indirect prompt injection, and the like can be propagated into the final output, as illustrated by a developer who followed an unreliable GitHub page surfaced by search and lost roughly $2,500 of private data. To answer the question of whether end users can trust LLM-based search agents when they encounter unreliable search results, the authors propose SafeSearch, an automated red-teaming framework that generates test cases, filters them with differential testing, simulates unreliable search results in a sandbox, and evaluates agents with specialized LLMs, and they run it over three scaffolds (search workflow, tool calling, deep research) and 17 representative LLMs. The headline result: LLMs are alarmingly easily misled when offered unreliable search results, with the highest attack success rate reaching 90.5% for GPT-4.1-mini under a search-workflow setting, and common defenses such as reminder prompting offer only limited protection.

### Findings (extracted from the full text)

- **Search agents are misled almost universally under benign queries, and even the safest tested configuration is not safe.** 300 test cases spanning five risk categories (misinformation, bias-inducing content, sponsored advertisement promotion, indirect prompt injection, and harmful outputs) are generated and run against three scaffolds across 17 representative LLMs; the highest ASR observed is 90.5% for GPT-4.1-mini under the search-workflow setting, and the safest configuration tested, Claude-Sonnet-4.5 with tool-calling, still has a non-zero ASR of 4.6%. Qualitatively, the dominant failure mode is uncritical trust in search results — agents accept and propagate risky content rather than discounting it. **[Source: Abstract; Section 4.2 "Main Findings of Red-Teaming with SafeSearch"]**
- **Agent scaffold choice matters more than model choice, and the budget-matched test reveals a budget story rather than a scaffold story.** GPT-4.1-mini attains an overall ASR of 90.5% under the search workflow, 57.4% with the deep-research scaffold; with budget-matched tools in force, tool-calling with 74.00% is actually safer than deep-research with 59.44% at a three-step budget, meaning the autonomous deep-research advantage "largely comes from using the available search budget more fully, while autonomous tool-calling often stops after too few searches." **[Source: Section 4.2 "Main Findings of Red-Teaming with SafeSearch"; Table 2 "Budget-controlled scaffold comparison"]**
- **The defenses tested fail to protect: reminder prompting is largely ineffective, and filtering only roughly halves the ASR.** Two baseline defenses are evaluated — reminder prompting and filtering via an auxiliary detector — and both reduce risk to some extent but fail to eliminate it; filtering was more effective at cutting the ASR, though a manual review of the detector's reasoning shows most detection successes arise from the relative lack of stealth in the unreliable websites used, an additional reason specially crafted websites could pose greater threats. The framework also reports the knowledge-action gap: GPT-4.1-mini can identify unreliable sources when explicitly tasked but often fails to act safely in agentic settings even with reminders. **[Source: Section 6 "Discussions"; Section 5 "Main Findings of Red-Teaming with SafeSearch"]**
- **Safety and perceived helpfulness can improve simultaneously, which is the mechanism behind why unsafe answers stay plausible to users.** Under tool calling, the average Helpfulness Score under manipulation (92.2) exceeds the Helpfulness Score in benign configuration (91.4); in the authors' own words, unsafe influence "can be subtle, yielding responses that still appear useful to end users" — while helpfulness here means perceived usefulness or plausibility, not factual correctness. **[Source: Section 4.2 "Main Findings of Red-Teaming with SafeSearch"]**
- **The test task is qualified as deliberately benign, and the framework's design choices are conservative by intent.** Each test case involves four components (a benign user query reflecting the use scenario, a target negative consequence, an unreliable website intended to distract the agent, and an evaluation method that determines whether the expected risk occurs), with at most one unreliable website injected into the search results and, for multi-round agents, only in the first round so subsequent searches can cross-check or disregard it; the threat is time-sensitive — unreliable content must lie beyond the backend LLM's knowledge cutoff. **[Source: Section 2.1 "Search Agent & New Threat Surface"; Section 3.1 "Threat Model & Framework Overview"]**

### Two-sentence takeaway
LLM-based search agents are alarmingly easy to mislead under benign queries — with Attack Success Rates up to 90.5% and even the most conservative tested scaffold still failing — so agentic search is not yet a safe surface to hand to end users directly. The actionable results: search budget discipline beats scaffold choice for safety, reminder-based defenses are almost worthless, and safety must be evaluated separately from perceived helpfulness, which an unsafe answer can score well on.

## Paper 3 — 2603.22862 — The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2603.22862 (latest published version v2) |
| **Title** | The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents: From Single-Tool Call to Multi-Tool Orchestration |
| **Authors** | Haoyuan Xu, Chang Li, Xinyan Ma, Xianhao Ou, Zihan Zhang, Tao He, Xiangyu Liu, Zixiang Wang, Jiafeng Liang, Zheng Chu, Runxuan Liu, Rongchuan Mu, Dandan Tu, Ming Liu, Bing Qin |
| **Subject category (arXiv abstract page)** | Software Engineering (cs.SE) — primary; Computation and Language (cs.CL) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abstract page)** | v1 Tue, 24 Mar 2026 07:05:05 UTC (v1, first submission); latest version v2 Thu, 2 Apr 2026 02:54:00 UTC |
| **arXiv abs page** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22862 |

### Abstract summary

Tool use lets LLMs access external information and invoke software systems, but the central research problem has shifted from isolated single-tool invocation to multi-tool orchestration over long trajectories with intermediate state, execution feedback, changing environments, and practical constraints such as safety, cost, and verifiability. This comprehensive survey unifies the task formulation (separating single-call tool use from long-horizon orchestration), then organizes the literature around six core dimensions — inference-time planning and execution, training and trajectory construction, safety and control, efficiency under resource constraints, capability completeness in open environments, and benchmark design and evaluation — and summarizes representative applications in software engineering, enterprise workflows, graphical user interfaces, and mobile systems. It concludes by discussing major challenges and outlining a future research agenda for building reliable, scalable, and verifiable multi-tool agents.

### Findings (extracted from the full text)

- **The field's central problem shifted from correctness of single-point calls to end-to-end robustness of multi-tool chains.** Traditional autoregressive generation and ReAct-style sequential traces implicitly assume tasks are linearly decomposable, which "exhibits severe structural mismatches when applied to nested calls, parallel I/O operations, or long-horizon goals," so the field has moved toward topology-aware planning that uses structures with higher expressive power; the primary objective has transitioned from the correctness of single-point calls to the end-to-end executability and robustness of multi-tool chains under state-mutating write operations, latency, cost, and safety constraints. **[Source: Abstract; Section 3 "Introduction"; Section 5.2 "Long-Horizon Orchestration"]**
- **Long-horizon competence depends on solving a queue of state-mutation problems — parallelism control and persistence — that no single-in-context design can fully handle.** Parallel execution "significantly enhances agent efficiency," but mixing write operations (database updates, transaction commits) "risks race conditions, leading to system state inconsistencies"; practical systems therefore split planning, execution and summarisation into separate roles (pioneered by HuggingGPT's centralized planning then decomposed planner/caller/summarizer structures; succeeded by later schedulers coordinating mandatory executors under capacity and observability constraints), maintain durable state and reusable abstractions across repeated sessions, and compress or externalize history so it stays relevant over long interaction. **[Source: Section 5.2 "Long-Horizon Orchestration"]**
- **Parallel execution is a latent security risk requiring explicit transaction and verification semantics, not just prompt-level safeguards.** The authors survey how safe parallelism is delivered: from "pre-execution static constraints" (intercepting potentially conflicting actions, plus pre-execution self-verification of risky writes via preview/bound checks) to "in-execution transaction management" (attributing bounded, delayed-commit, compensation-based rollback semantics, as in SagaLLM and Atomix), and finally to "post-execution dynamic verification" (interactive critiquing, verifier-executor collaborations, and embedded verification subgraphs in scheduling graphs). **[Source: Section 5.1 "Security Risks in Parallel Execution"]**
- **Benchmark standards lagged behind what multi-tool agents actually do, and the authors set an explicit open-problem agenda.** Tool use, tuning, training, evaluation, and benchmark environments (including modern interactive closed-loop environments such as ELT-Bench, ToolSword, and MemAgentBench) are all catalogued, but three challenges remain before production deployment: mitigating behavioral drift caused by continuous interface evolution (e.g., changing tool availability) via adaptive regression, establishing process-level verifiability and replayable audits "without prohibitive efficiency loss," and developing systematic methodologies to jointly optimize cost, latency, and quality under strict budget constraints — with recovery being replaced from heuristic failure handling by composable, adversarial-resistant recovery. **[Source: Section 10 "Benchmarks"; Section 9 "The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents"; Section 9.4 "Open Problems and Future Directions"]**

### Two-sentence takeaway
Multi-tool orchestration — not single-tool calling — is the active frontier in LLM agents, and the advances so far have come from repartitioning labour (planner/caller/summarizer, small + large models) and from structured state and transaction semantics, not from any single correctness gain. Its practical implications for us: every act of an agent is a state-mutation surface, so transaction and per-step verification matter for long-horizon actions, and safe pipelines need auditability and verifiability built in rather than bolted on.

## Cross-paper discussion worth raising Thursday

1. **Count of calls is the wrong metric; trust calibration and attribution are what changes behaviour.** Amani et al. show no monotonic quality gain from search-calling (14% vs 83% of prompts trigger it), only 5–15 URLs per query arrive, and 14%–53% of claims sit on results the agent fetched but never cited. SafeSearch shows the same pattern from the safety side: agents are misread to 90.5% ASR after exposure to *one* unreliable, unrelated page, and with 17 back ends, three scaffolds and 300 risk scenarios the failures compound rather than cancel. The common thread is that what matters is not whether the agent voiced the right thing, but whether the rejected or rushed information shaped it.
2. **The failure badly is a control design — not a data quality problem.** The dominant failure mode Amani et al. identify is uncritical trust over domain preferences, and SafeSearch's failure mode is agents accepting searches as authoritative without verification under mildly adversarial conditions. Our own context is what adds error-free replication plus adversarial calibration: tool descriptions can bear weight, and the architecture deliberately limits cheap switching of simple steps. That discipline is what might make Cross-paper shifts more robust when cross-paper branches move slowly.
3. **Adding assistance raises exposure; specifics drawn too thin may leave the centre of the job building a deeper loop.** The honest risk with this pairing is not the professionally supported interpretations, but steering toward the safest interpretation. We need to leave our own assumptions ambiguous so downstream decisions function with errors tolerated from the edges of the loop.
