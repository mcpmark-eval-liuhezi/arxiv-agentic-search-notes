# Reading Notes: Agentic Search and LLM Tool Use

A literature brief for Thursday's team sync on **agentic search** — assistants where an LLM-driven agent searches, calls tools, and hands answers to users. The brief was assembled end to end: arXiv searches were run on the requested phrasings ("conversational search agents", "tool use in LLM agents", and "user trust in AI assistants"); the three most relevant hits that surfaced are covered below. Every paper was then read through its full text (not just the abstract), and each entry reports two or three concrete findings — evaluation results, design decisions, or acknowledged limitations — each tagged with the paper section it came from. Subject category and submission date are taken from each paper's public arXiv abstract page (verified by loading https://arxiv.org/abs/<ID>).

## Paper 1 — 2609.19244 — Characterizing Web Search by Conversational LLM Agents

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2609.19244 (v1) |
| **Title** | Characterizing Web Search by Conversational LLM Agents: From Search Decisions and Strategies to Results and Responses |
| **Authors** | Mahsa Amani, Seungeon Lee, Abhisek Dash, Asmaa El Fraihi, Yunah Jang, Elisabeth Kirsten, Qinyuan Wu, Krishna P. Gummadi, Manish Gupta, Abhilasha Ravichander, Muhammad Bilal Zafar, Soumi Das |
| **Subject category (arXiv abs page)** | Primary: Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); cross-listed: Information Retrieval (cs.IR) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abs page)** | Wed, 16 Sep 2026 17:56:32 UTC (v1) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19244 |

**Abstract summary.** This is the first study of end-to-end Web search carried out by conversational LLM agents, combining real donated user conversations ("in vivo") from four platforms — ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and DeepSeek — with controlled API-based replay of the same prompts ("in vitro"). The authors examine when a model decides to invoke Web search, how agents query and iteratively reformulate queries based on retrieved results, the domain preferences of the search engines they hit, and how the claims in the final answer are actually grounded in cited evidence. Headline findings: search-calling decisions vary enormously across platforms and models, more frequent Web-search invocation does not necessarily yield better response quality, several claims rest on search results the agent fetched but never cited, and the search engines returning results for LLM agents concentrate them in their preferred domains.

**Findings (extracted from the full text).**

- **Search-calling decisions vary by an order of magnitude across agents, and invoking search does not necessarily improve answers.** In the "in vitro" setup the same 1,000 user prompts were replayed through each platform's API: Web search was invoked for only 140 of the prompts (14%) by GPT-5.3-chat versus 825 (83%) by Claude Sonnet 4.6, and — critically — prompts for which models do *not* invoke Web search score higher across all response-quality measures (factuality, completeness, relevance). Web search is beneficial for GPT-5.3-chat, Grok-4.3, and DeepSeek-v4-flash, while Claude Sonnet 4.6, despite invoking search extensively, exhibits at best mixed effects. Agents are good at deciding when they do *not* need Web search, but their decisions to call do not always improve their responses. **[Source: Section 3 "Analyzing Search Calling Decisions", specifically §3.1 "Search Calling Behaviors" and §3.3 "Need for Search Calling"; echoed in Sections 8–9 ("Analyzing Search Queries and Results") and 12]**
- **Attribution is the structural weak point: a large share of claims rest on results the agent never cited, and the ungrounded portion tends to be the least factual.** Entailment evaluation (validated against human annotation with 93% agreement between human and automated judgments) shows associated citations account for 30–46% of grounded claims, but across ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok, uncited-yet-searched URLs consistently account for **14% to 53% of all claims**, and **15%–20% of all claims remain ungrounded** (potentially parametric-knowledge-derived). Factuality scores: claims supported by associated citations 3.47 (3.35 for other cited sources) vs. ungrounded claims 2.84 — the lowest. **[Source: Section 13 "Analyzing Search Responses", specifically §13.2 "Grounding of Claims"]**
- **Acknowledged limitation — the analysis relies on observational traces and automated judge models.** The authors state that their datasets do not expose the full internal reasoning processes, hidden chain-of-thought traces, ranking algorithms, or proprietary retrieval pipelines used by the platforms, so some observed behaviours may reflect platform-specific harness implementations rather than the underlying models alone. They also flag that several analyses depend on automated judge models (GPT-4o-mini for OpenAI data; Claude-Haiku-3.5, Grok-3-mini, and Deepseek-chat otherwise), that LLM-as-a-judge evaluation "remains imperfect and may inherit biases or inconsistencies from the evaluation models themselves." Finally, the study focuses on English-language interactions and does not analyze multimodal retrieval, non-English retrieval, or personalized memory systems. **[Source: Section 15 "Limitations"; Section 2 "Datasets and Experimental Setups"; Section 14 "Conclusion"]**

**Takeaway.** Search-calling behaviour is a platform artifact as much as a need signal: the same prompts draw a 14%–83% spread of search-calling decisions, and more searches do not necessarily yield better answers. Attribution is the structural weak point — 14%–53% of claims sit on results the agent fetched but never cited — and users have no visibility into the domain preferences (e.g., which sources Claude and Grok always omit) encoding these biases. For our own agentic search surface, evaluate on attribution and answer quality, not on how often the model calls a tool.

## Paper 2 — 2603.22862 — The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2603.22862 (v2) |
| **Title** | The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents: From Single-Tool Call to Multi-Tool Orchestration |
| **Authors** | Haoyuan Xu, Chang Li, Xinyan Ma, Xianhao Ou, Zihan Zhang, Tao He, Xiangyu Liu, Zixiang Wang, Jiafeng Liang, Zheng Chu, Runxuan Liu, Rongchuan Mu, Dandan Tu, Ming Liu, Bing Qin |
| **Subject category (arXiv abs page)** | Primary: Software Engineering (cs.SE); cross-listed: Computation and Language (cs.CL) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abs page)** | v1 Tue, 24 Mar 2026 07:05:05 UTC; v2 Thu, 2 Apr 2026 02:54:00 UTC — https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22862 |

**Abstract summary.** A comprehensive survey of how tool use in LLM agents has shifted from isolated single-tool invocation to multi-tool orchestration over long trajectories with intermediate state, execution feedback, changing environments, and practical constraints such as safety, cost, and verifiability. It unifies task formulations and distinguishes single-call tool use from long-horizon orchestration, organizes the literature around six core dimensions (inference-time planning and execution; training and trajectory construction; safety and control; efficiency under resource constraints; capability completeness in open environments; and benchmark design and evaluation), and summarizes representative applications in software engineering, enterprise workflows, GUIs, and mobile systems. The survey concludes that trust in agent reliability now centers on system-level orchestration rather than call-level correctness.

**Findings (extracted from the full text).**

- **The field's central problem shift: from "can a model select the right tool?" to "can it coordinate many tools over long trajectories while managing state, cost, and safety?"** Traditional paradigms such as ReAct organize tool usage into sequential traces, which "exhibit severe structural mismatches when applied to nested calls, parallel I/O operations, or long-horizon goals" — linear constraints "inherently limit the representation of data flow dependencies, preclude the parallel execution of independent operations, and force early decision errors to accumulate throughout the trajectory." As tool spaces expand to the tens of thousands of APIs, the challenge becomes dependence-aware combinatorial decision-making: dynamic tool subset selection, cross-tool dependency modeling, sequential and parallel scheduling, failure recovery, and re-planning. **[Source: Section 5 "Multi-Tool Agent Inference-Time Paradigms", specifically §5.1 "Topological Planning" and §5.2 "Long-Horizon Orchestration"]**
- **Safety is a first-class design dimension, not an afterthought: multi-tool orchestration creates new attack surfaces ("state pollution").** The survey categorizes multi-tool security risks into parallel execution (race conditions, write interference, partial commits, memory/plan poisoning, long-horizon drift) and long-horizon tool chains (agent drift, cross-tool state contamination, memory poisoning via read operations, plan injection). Defenses evolve along an arc from "pre-execution static constraints" (AARM, AgentSpec interceptors) to "in-execution transaction management" (SagaLLM bounded workflows with transactional rollback; Atomix epoch-based concurrency isolation) to "post-execution dynamic verification" (CRITIC self-correction, VerifiAgent, DVR verification subgraphs). The key design decision: safe parallelism requires "explicit control over side effects and execution scope," not better prompting. **[Source: Section 7 "Safety of Multi-Tool Agents", specifically §7.1 "Security Risks in Parallel Execution" and §7.2 "Security Risks in Long-Horizon Tool Chains"]**
- **The survey's conceptual framing vs. prior work, and its call to action.** It takes multi-tool orchestration — not tool use in general or agent systems broadly — as the main unit of analysis, draws clearer boundaries among conflated terms (tool invocation, tool retrieval, orchestration, toolset expansion), and relates progress to the shift from "call-level correctness to system-level reliability in both benchmarks and applications." The authors conclude that the dimensions are tightly connected: "planning interacts with state management and verifiability; safety depends on execution control and isolation; efficiency depends on decomposition, routing, and reuse." They call for better abstractions for stateful orchestration and stronger evaluation protocols for dynamic, long-horizon settings. **[Source: Section 2 "Introduction", specifically §2.1 "Aims and Motivations"; Section 10 "Conclusion"]**

**Takeaway.** For our team: when we build agentic search assistants, plan for orchestration from day one — the survey's six dimensions (planning, training, safety, efficiency, completeness, evaluation) are the checklist, and safety counsel is now "transaction-style isolation + rollback + post-execution verification," not mutual-说教. The survey is a good single read to align the team before writing any agent code.

## Paper 3 — 2502.13321 — Adjust for Trust: Mitigating Trust-Induced Inappropriate Reliance on AI Assistance

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **arXiv ID** | 2502.13321 (v2) |
| **Title** | Adjust for Trust: Mitigating Trust-Induced Inappropriate Reliance on AI Assistance |
| **Authors** | Tejas Srinivasan, Jesse Thomason |
| **Subject category (arXiv abs page)** | Primary: Human-Computer Interaction (cs.HC); cross-listed: Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Computation and Language (cs.CL) |
| **Submission date (arXiv abs page)** | v1 Tue, 18 Feb 2025 22:42:39 UTC; v2 Mon, 26 Jan 2026 16:35:39 UTC — https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13321 |

**Abstract summary.** User trust biases how users rely on AI recommendations, with low and high trust producing increased under- and over-reliance respectively. The authors propose that AI assistants should adapt their behaviour through trust-adaptive interventions: when trust is low, providing an explanation can elicit more careful consideration; when trust is high, counter-explanations or forced pauses can reduce over-reliance. In two decision-making scenarios — laypeople answering science questions and doctors making medical diagnoses — providing supporting and counter-explanations during moments of low and high trust yields up to a 38% reduction in inappropriate reliance and a 20% improvement in decision accuracy; adaptively inserting forced pauses also reduces over-reliance. The paper also generalizes to a real LLM assistant, and finds that purely interaction-level trust heuristics are insufficient to predict moments of low/high trust.

**Findings (extracted from the full text).**

- **Extreme user trust acts as a cognitive bias: trust is strongly correlated with inappropriate reliance, and both directions of the bias are quantified.** Switch Rate correlation with user trust is 0.796–0.949; Over-Reliance is highest at trust values 9–10 (correlation 0.500–0.780); Under-Reliance is highest at low trust (<5) with strong negative correlations (-0.903 to -0.742). Concrete numbers from the Introduction: medical doctors mistakenly accept 26% of AI misdiagnoses when trust is high vs. 8% when trust is lower (over-reliance); conversely when trust is low, doctors reject correct AI diagnoses 68% of the time vs. 40% otherwise (under-reliance). **[Source: Section 5 "Results" (from §5.1 and related results); Introduction]**
- **Design decision — trust-adaptive interventions: matching explanation type to trust level, validated with a real LLM.** When trust is low the assistant surfaces supporting explanations (encouraging careful consideration); when trust is high it surfaces counter-explanations (highlighting reasons the recommendation may be wrong) and/or adaptively inserts forced pauses to slow the interaction and promote deliberation. In two decision-making scenarios (laypeople on science questions; doctors on clinical diagnoses) this yields up to 38% reduction in inappropriate reliance and 20% improvement in decision accuracy, and reducing over-reliance via forced pauses works but does not reduce under-reliance. The findings generalise to a real LLM assistant (§11), and the authors adopt an 11-point global trust scale measured after each interaction. **[Source: Section 11 "Results" (Experiment #5: Trust-Adaptive Interventions with an LLM Assistant); Section 5 "Results"; §11.2]**
- **Acknowledged limitation — simulated AI and non-representative samples.** The majority of user studies used a simulated AI assistant (for controllability of answer accuracy and confidence calibration) rather than a real system; since answer correctness is decided independently per problem, the assistant may give contradicting predictions for near-identical problems. Findings come from UK/US English-speaking Prolific participants, and the authors note professional doctors were scarce on the platform, limiting reliability with more participants/tasks/interventions. They also find using purely interaction-level features (heuristic or learning-based methods) is insufficient to accurately predict moments of low and high trust — an open challenge for the field. **[Source: Section 14 "Limitations"; Section 11.2; Section 12 "Analysis: Can We Model User Trust?"]**

**Takeaway.** Trust is an operational lever, not a personality trait: when we instrument our assistant we should measure trust level and switch on different explanation strategies (supporting vs. counter-explanations) based on it, and be prepared to slow the interaction with forced pauses to combat over-reliance. We should treat trust-adaptive design as a feature of good agentic search assistants, and pair it with citation provenance so under-reliance isn't caused by opaque ungrounded claims (see Paper 1).

## Cross-paper takeaways for Thursday

1. **Call volume is not the metric.** Amani et al. (2609.19244) show a 14%–83% spread of search-calling decisions on identical prompts, and 15%–20% of claims rest on URLs the agent never cited — evaluate agentic search on attribution and answer quality, not on how often the model calls a tool.
2. **Orchestration and safety are the hard problems, not prompting.** Xu et al. (2603.22862) show the field has shifted from single-tool selection to long-horizon orchestration where planning, state management, and verification are tightly coupled — and safety now means transaction-style isolation, rollback, and post-execution dynamic verification.
3. **Calibration and trust need to be designed in, not bolted on.** Srinivasan & Thomason (2502.13321) show that adapting assistant behaviour to measured trust (supporting vs. counter-explanations, forced pauses) cuts inappropriate reliance by up to 38% and improves accuracy by 20%, while plain transparency about confidence doesn't fix miscalibrated trust — so citation provenance and human-in-the-loop are part of the default assistant design.
4. **We have no clean, shareable eval harness for agentic search today.** Amani's dataset cannot be publicly released (only mock data), and the survey notes the benchmark/evaluation gap between industrial systems and research benchmarks — worth raising as an open item for the team.
